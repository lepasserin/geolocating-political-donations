REVIEWER #2
-----------

Reviewer #2: I am very grateful to have been given a chance to review this paper. This represents a major contribution to the literature on campaign donations and campaign finance. Once this is published, I will be citing this in my own scholarship. The quality of the data is impressive, the manuscript is very clear and well written, and the analysis is meticulous, well conceived, and properly executed. I considered submitting a recommendation to "accept" as is, but I do have a few suggestions for minor revisions. I don't anticipate it would take more than a couple of hours to resolve my concerns .

(1) This paper will be of interest to students of US elections and campaign finance. For those unfamiliar with Canadian politics, what is a "district association"? How does this relate to "localized entities"? A simple explanation of a sentence or two should suffice.

  - Added two sentences to the Introduction defining electoral district associations and the term localized entities.

(2) A bit of caution about interpreting the results of the Individual-level model. Here, the DV is whether or not a donation was sent out-of-district. Donations are linked to a specific individual, about whom we have very limited information. Instead the author(s) use district-level socio-economic and demographic data. I'm OK with this, but please make clear that this approach means that you are drawing inferences about individual behavior based on the district as a whole. (Forgive me if my interpretation of this analysis is flawed.) As correctly noted in the discussion section, this approach may explain why we observe donations coming from districts with larger visible minority populations are more likely to flow out-of-state. Please be clear about the implications of this finding. On the one hand, as mentioned in the review, some minority groups actually do have a higher propensity to give, but given the correlation between population density and visible minority
populations, the study cannot provide definitive evidence that individuals from visible minority groups are actually giving out of district at higher rates. Nevertheless, it is an interesting finding that is worth further study in the future.

  - Added a sentence to Section 3.3.1 stating that inference from the model to individual donors is ecological.
  - Added a sentence to the Discussion stating that the results do not establish that visible minority individuals give out-of-district at higher rates, only that donations from highly minoritized neighbourhoods do.

(3) Page 22 (of the PDF generated by the journal) references "smaller" districts. I take it to mean that this refers to geographic/spacial size, since the population density variable is included in the model. I'm assuming that the decision not to include constituency population size (logged scale is typically best) is because federal ridings (at least within provinces) are approximately equal. But across provinces/terrorities, there is substantial malapportionment. For example, the difference between Newfoundland and the Edmonton—Wetaskiwin ridings are nearly 10:1. Research of Keena (2019; doi:10.1080/07343469.2019.1572673) shows that population size affects campaign fundraising in important ways that have a direct implication for out-of-district money. You may want to put some more thought and discussion into the complex interaction between geographic size, population size, urbanization, and diversity in your Section 3-4 models to identify which variable(s) are really
important in driving outcomes.

  - "smaller districts" is now "geographically smaller districts".
  - Added two sentences on why district population was not included as a covariate, citing Keena (2019). Added Keena (2019) to references.bib.
  - Fuller treatment of size, population, urbanization, and diversity: not yet done

(4) I hate to be nitpick here, but I think it may be better report estimates (Tables 5 and 6) using significant digits, rather than a fixed decimal points. This is standard practice in the physical sciences, and it is highlighted by the problem with the significant effect of income in Table 5 and 6 (0.000). This implies that the effect is small, but that would be wrong. We know that income is hugely important for driving donation outcomes. It would be better to report each effect in terms of the number of significant digits (which is based no the quality and precision of the data available, not the size of the number). Also, a bigger issue with income is how the number is formatted is how to interpret the effect of money. For table 5, for example, we are looking at the increase in likelihood of a donation going out-of-district that is associated with a one-dollar increase in median income of a district. If this is the case, perhaps you can reformat this to "thousands" of
dollars because we don't need that level of precision in the model! Note that it should not affect the overall performance of the model if you simply covert your median income values to thousands other than the reported magnitude (and standard error) associated with this particular variable. (You will also need to reprint Figures 5 and 6.)

  - Median household income is now in thousands of dollars in all models; table and plot labels now read "Income (thousands)"; variable definitions updated. Figures 5 and 6 regenerate on render.
  - Significant-digit change: not yet done

(5) Something for the discussion: What does this say about the health of Canadian democracy? Does it tell us anything about the quality of localized representation at the level of the federal riding? The US case may not be a very good baseline, but it makes Canadian democracy seem a lot healthier in terms of the role of money flowing into the national legislature. Just a thought!

  - Added one sentence to the Discussion regionalization paragraph on the health of local representation in Canada.


REVIEWER #3
-----------

Reviewer #3: This study conducts an empirical examination of 'out-of-district' donations in Canada. Despite civic participation in federal elections being rooted in single member voting districts, the author(s) uncover significant amounts of donations going to EDAs / candidates ('localized entities') from individuals who reside in another voting district. Out-of-district donations account for about 40% of the individuals who made a donation and the amounts ($) being raised by localized entities. The study thus aims to contribute to a literature on out-of-district donations to federal congressional candidates in the United States.

The empirical analysis of this article is quite thorough. The very detailed data cleaning report addresses typical concerns about removing duplicates from quarterly vs annual reports, and errors these data are prone to contain because they are often submitted by volunteer agents for localized entities. The author(s) have clearly done a significant amount of work to pair Elections Canada data with Canada Post data and census data. The paper also has some findings. One is that most (though not all) of the out-of-district (OOD) donations go to localized entities in an adjacent district. Another is that districts with higher proportions of socio-cultural diversity correlate with the odds of making an OOD donation.

A major limitation of the study is rooted in what can be gleaned from these data of contributions from individuals. The original hook -- that electoral participation is confined to districts but donations are not -- is an important first step but it must go further to explain why this is occurring.  Studies from the USA are worthy of publication because they test the motives and / or consequences of OOD donations. The study by Gimpel et al. is notable because it assesses the types of candidates who rely more on funding from OOD donations (i.e., new challengers vs incumbents, seniority in Congressional leadership, whether the candidate is from an under-represented group such as women, black, Latino, 2SLGBTQ+). Others test to see if OOD donations impact the ideological position of candidates / elected officials (Jacobs and Imboywa 2024) relative to their districts or whether citizens perceive a cost in terms of their own interests (not) being represented when their congress
person relies on OOD donations (Nathan et al. 2025). Put differently, the literature on OOD donations goes beyond documenting their existence to theorizing why they occur and testing those theory with additional data.

  - not yet done

Given the relative ease of doing something similar with existing public datasets for Canada, I am inclined to see this study published if it can, upon revision, tease out why these contributions occur. One way to do this is to see if there is variation in the kinds of candidates who receive OOD donations. Other public datasets provide data for election candidate gender, racial / Indigenous background, and sexuality (Johnston et al.'s Candidate Demographic Dataset in this journal; Baisley and Quinn's dataset on 2SLGBTQ+ candidates also in this journal). Do these types of candidates rely less (more) than their white male counterparts? If so, what does this mean for representation in Canadian politics? Or perhaps candidates from racialized backgrounds rely more on OOD donations because their community is rooted in a neighbouring district. The author(s) could pair their database of localized entities to probe for an affinity affect given the odds of an OOD donation from
districts where high proportions of "Visible Minority."

  - not yet done

Another angle for the recipients is whether OOD donations flow from less competitive districts to more competitive districts or richer districts to poorer districts. This is another theory from the USA literature, and performing this analysis could help make sense of the positive correlation between Margin of Victory in the sender's district and an OOD.  Perhaps OOD donations are being sent from individuals in a district their preferred party is likely to lose (guaranteed to win) and so they donate to candidates in neighbouring districts where purchase power is stronger. They could also see if OOD donations flow into open districts (no incumbent reoffering) to see if these are to help challengers.

  - not yet done

To summarize, this is a great dataset and an interesting project. But in my view the existing study doesn't push far enough into theorizing (then testing) why OOD donations occur to warrant publication as a research article. The study finds that the lion's share of OOD donations often go to localized entities in the neighbouring district (Table 4, page 20), and that it correlates with some demography, but I'm don't know why we should care about this patterns without knowing which candidates / parties in competitive contexts benefit as recipients. Do donors in big cities really know they're donating to a candidate in another riding when that riding boundary is only several blocks away?  The author(s) could address this major limitation by revising their study to examine where OOD donations are going beyond the aggregate party level and neighbouring districts/out of province districts. It would be quite manageable to pair this dataset with other publicly available datasets to
address who receives this money / the districts where OOD donations are going, thus resulting in a clearer sense of why these types of donations occur.

  - not yet done
